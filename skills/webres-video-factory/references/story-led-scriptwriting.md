# Story-led scriptwriting and visual direction

Use this resource in Phase 2 of Webres Video Factory when developing or revising a short-form video. Its job is to connect the spoken story to camera coverage, edit rhythm, on-screen text, evidence, and purposeful motion before Flow prompts or motion graphics are produced. Keep execution details such as voice generation, word-clock timing, rendering, and audio mastering in the main Webres Video Factory skill.

This is a decision framework, not a guaranteed-virality formula. A retention device is useful only when it strengthens the story, helps comprehension, or gives the viewer a reason to continue.

## 1. Start with the viewer's question and the evidence

Before writing lines, define:

- **Audience and context:** who should understand this, and what do they already know?
- **Viewer question:** what would make this topic worth their attention now?
- **Promise:** what will the viewer understand, see, or be able to decide by the end?
- **Evidence:** which real source, interface, demonstration, or example supports each claim?
- **Boundary:** what is unknown, experimental, region-limited, or otherwise easy to overstate?
- **Action:** what single response should the viewer take after watching?
- **Format:** platform, aspect ratio, target duration, available footage, presenter, brand assets, and any supplied references.

Verify factual claims using `facts.md` and primary sources as required by the main skill. A visual can illustrate a fact but cannot stand in for evidence. Separate confirmed facts, interpretation, and creative metaphor in the plan.

When the user supplies video references, inspect them before scripting if access permits. Record observable choices rather than vague labels: opening image and line, shot sizes, camera movement, approximate cut rhythm, when text enters, how graphics relate to titles, use of product proof, sound accents, and how the ending asks for action. Separate those observations from the new video's own solution; adapt mechanics, not another creator's exact footage, wording, or distinctive design.

## 2. Build the narrative before counting lines

Shape the story around its actual logic. A useful pattern for an explainer is:

1. **Hook:** present the question, surprising image, tension, or result immediately.
2. **Orientation:** tell the viewer what the subject is and why this example matters.
3. **Development:** explain the mechanism or steps in a sequence that is easy to follow.
4. **Proof or demonstration:** show the real interface, action, comparison, or result being discussed.
5. **Qualification or implication:** state a meaningful limitation, condition, or consequence when needed.
6. **CTA:** ask for one specific action that follows naturally from the topic.

This pattern is optional. Reorder, combine, or omit beats when the subject has a stronger structure. Every spoken line should advance the story; remove greetings, repeated setup, and filler. Do not make a false promise, exaggerate certainty, or withhold essential context just to create an open loop.

## 3. Give every shot group its own problem-hook-solution arc

Do not rely on the full video having one opening hook and one ending solution. Treat **each group of related shots** as a small, complete story:

1. **Problem:** show a concrete friction, question, risk, limitation, or unmet need in the subject of this group.
2. **Hook:** sharpen why this problem matters or create a specific curiosity about its cause or next step. Make the hook visible and/or audible within the group; do not use a generic attention phrase.
3. **Solution:** pay off that problem with the explanation, demonstration, decision, or result before the group ends.

The first group must also introduce the whole video's subject and promise immediately. Later groups repeat the local problem → hook → solution arc while advancing the larger story. Keep the three parts concise; they may span several shots and need not be three separate spoken sentences. The visual can carry one part while the presenter speaks another, but their relationship must be clear.

Before scripting, divide the video into meaningful groups and write a one-line problem, hook, and solution for each. Reject a group that only shows a product, repeats an earlier point, or adds a transition without resolving a local question. End each group with a visual or spoken payoff that earns the cut into the next group's problem. Do not prolong an unresolved tease across groups just to manufacture retention.

## 4. Use five retention devices as planning questions

Consider each device; use only those that fit. They are prompts for editorial choices, not mandatory decorations. This five-part framing is adapted from [a creator's Instagram example](https://www.instagram.com/p/DbuMGnkx-e9/); it is not a promise of virality or a template to copy literally.

### Unexpected visual

Can the first image contain a relevant object, action, contrast, or product result that catches the eye before the viewer scrolls? Keep it immediately legible and pay it off in the story. A surprising prop unrelated to the subject is visual noise. If no prop fits, use a strong product image, human gesture, or carefully chosen framing change.

### Strong hook

Put the main curiosity or benefit in the first spoken beat and first screen. Pair the visual with the spoken idea; do not let the title explain one thing while the image suggests another. Prefer a concrete question, result, or tension over a generic greeting or brand intro. State the promised answer clearly enough that the viewer knows why to stay.

### Useful information

Give each beat one main idea and a reason to believe or care about it. When describing a product function, show that function; when making a claim, show its source or qualification. Avoid repeating the same claim in narration, headline, subtitle, and icon. Keep enough screen time for both the evidence and the words to register.

### Pattern break

Mark stretches that would otherwise repeat the same shot, framing, delivery, or visual treatment. Break the pattern with a motivated change: presenter to product evidence, close-up to wider context, a meaningful action, a title reveal, or a graphic response to a word. Choose the break from the reference rhythm and story beat. Do not add a transition, flash, movement, or sound merely because a preset exists. A purposeful held image can restore contrast after a quick sequence.

### Clear CTA

End with one direct, topic-specific action: comment with an idea, save a useful explanation, try a tool, or follow a relevant link. Choose the action before finalizing the ending, then leave enough visual and spoken time to understand it. Avoid stacking unrelated requests such as like, comment, share, follow, and visit a site in one breath.

## 5. Write the spoken script and shot plan as a pair

For each spoken line or edit beat, make a shot plan before generating or animating assets. The spoken script should remain natural to say aloud; visual instructions belong in the edit plan or production notes, not inside dialogue.

Use a table with these fields:

| Field | Decision to record |
|---|---|
| Shot group / arc role | Group identifier and whether this beat states the local problem, sharpens the hook, or pays it off with the solution. Every group must contain all three. |
| Beat / anchor | Line ID and the word or phrase that motivates the cut or graphic event. |
| Story function | Hook, orientation, explanation, proof, qualification, pattern break, or CTA. |
| Spoken copy | One clear idea, fact-checked and comfortable to say at the target pace. |
| Visual evidence | Presenter, real product footage, screen capture, licensed insert, or clearly marked illustration. |
| Shot design | Size, angle, subject position, camera movement or crop, and the visual focus. |
| Cut logic | Why the shot starts here, what changes, and whether a hard cut, match cut, or transition serves the meaning. |
| Text hierarchy | Headline, subtitle, data/credit; exact wording, relative size, weight, brand accent, safe position, and dwell time. |
| Graphic action | What the graphic explains or emphasizes, where it is placed, how it connects to the title/product, and its motion states. |
| Retention function | Which of the five devices above this beat serves, if any. |
| Audio cue | Voice, music, or a specific transient only when the visual action warrants it. |

Do not assume every spoken line needs a new camera setup. One source take can support several motivated crops or inserts, but never manufacture a hands-only or generic laptop shot just to create coverage. If a planned camera angle or action cannot be generated or found in the available material, redesign the shot before production rather than implying that it exists.

## 6. Direct camera coverage and edit rhythm

Assign shot size by communication purpose:

- **Close-up:** intimacy, a key question, reaction, or a precise emotional turn. Preserve eye contact and facial expression.
- **Medium shot:** explanation, natural gestures, and presenter-to-product context.
- **Wide or establishing shot:** location, spatial context, or the relationship between person and environment.
- **Product insert:** the exact interface state or action mentioned in the narration. Retain enough surrounding UI to establish that it is genuine and understandable.
- **Detail insert:** a physical action only when it carries information; do not use hands, typing, or device beauty shots as generic filler.

Vary coverage to create a visual sequence, not a repeated zoom. Keep screen direction, eyeline, body position, light, and wardrobe coherent across adjacent cuts. Use facial performance and gesture appropriate to the idea; camera energy should not make the presenter feel stiff, exaggerated, or detached from the words.

Choose shot duration from speech density, action, evidence, and reference rhythm. Use a quick insert for a quick visual fact, but hold a product demonstration long enough to see what changed. Cut on a spoken anchor, gesture, reveal, or action when possible. Use J/L audio overlaps to preserve verbal continuity across a visual cut. A hard cut is the default when it communicates cleanly; use an effect transition only when it expresses a meaningful relationship. Vary pace: a short burst of cuts can earn a purposeful hold, and a longer explanation can be punctuated by proof or a reframing.

## 7. Balance the frame: picture, headline, subtitle, and graphic

Establish a composition per shot using the actual 9:16 image, not an empty mock-up. Decide where the face, product UI, headline, captions, and supporting graphic can coexist without competing. Protect platform interface zones and keep every text line inside the frame's safe area.

Use distinct typographic roles:

- **Headline:** the idea of the shot, short enough to read at phone size and visually dominant.
- **Subtitle:** the spoken information, broken into readable phrases and visually subordinate to the headline.
- **Data, source, or limitation:** supporting information, displayed only as large and long as comprehension requires.

Do not duplicate a caption as a headline unless the repetition intentionally adds emphasis. Preserve the chosen font, regular/bold contrast, and brand accent throughout the sequence. If brand-colored bold text loses contrast over footage, first move it to negative space, adjust the surrounding background, or add a restrained local scrim; do not silently recolor the brand word. On product shots, keep source UI legible and use any surrounding gradient or color treatment to support the product palette without obscuring the interface.

Check visible glyphs, not just CSS boxes: descenders, accents, shadows, and line spacing must not be clipped. Review long and short titles, every line break, and every scene boundary. Keep titles stable long enough to read; do not shrink a headline merely to preserve a fixed location when a better line break or position solves the problem.

Aim for one dominant focal point and, at most, one supporting graphic at a time. If the viewer must choose between reading the title, watching the presenter, and inspecting a detailed interface, simplify or sequence those elements.

## 8. Give motion a semantic relationship

For every animated graphic, write its job in one sentence before implementation: what does it point to, reveal, compare, transform, or explain? Connect its path, shape, timing, or placement to the spoken word, title, gesture, or product state. A small icon parked in a corner is not an interaction.

Use a motion sequence with intentional state changes: **rest or anticipation → spoken trigger → reveal / travel / transformation → settle or hold → exit or reset**. Continuous movement is not required; planned stillness can make the next action clearer. Give the animation enough time to read and keep its motion subordinate to the main idea.

When the brief asks for an existing LottieFiles animation, source the actual animation, inspect its timing and license, preserve its original motion, record its creator/source, and place it where its meaning meets the title or product. Add a complementary connector or reveal only when it makes that relationship clearer. Do not redraw the existing asset as a generic icon, reuse prior icons when asked for new assets, or add decorative dots, glints, drift, or loops without a narrative purpose. Use word-clock anchors for meaningful triggers; keep `renderAt(t)` deterministic as required by WVF.

## 9. Build a low-cost visual proof before full production

Before final voice generation or full animation, prepare a concise, reviewable proof appropriate to the stage: a shot table, storyboard/contact sheet, or a few representative frames showing the opening, product treatment, pattern break, and ending. It should expose framing, title scale, color, and text/image balance early enough to change them cheaply.

After the edit is assembled, inspect a contact sheet covering every shot and watch the video at normal speed on a phone-sized view. Check each cut and the frames around it for:

- an opening image and hook that land immediately;
- visible and correctly framed face, product, and evidence;
- purposeful shot changes and a rhythm that varies without losing comprehension;
- headline hierarchy, brand styling, legibility, safe margins, and adequate dwell time;
- no stale title, repeated callout, clipped text, filler insert, or unsupported graphic;
- motion that relates to the words or picture and includes enough visual rest;
- a CTA that is specific, readable, and supported by the final spoken line.

Translate any recurring review issue into a check over every relevant shot, not only the timestamp where it was first noticed. A clean technical QA report does not replace this visual review.

## 10. Worked planning example

For a line such as “Mira esta demo: escriben la idea de una cafetería, con una taza que atrapa ingredientes,” plan the shot sequence as evidence, not as generic B-roll:

1. Open on the real prompt or game state that creates curiosity; keep the hook title in a clear zone.
2. Cut to the actual prompt as the narration names the café; retain enough UI to show what the person entered.
3. Reveal the generated café game when the narration describes the cup; hold the playable result long enough to inspect.
4. If a coffee-cup Lottie is requested, preserve its animation and connect its movement to the bold title or the visible cup, rather than leaving it detached in a corner.
5. Cut to the next state when the voice says the design appears; do not leave the previous title or callout over the new scene.

The exact number and duration of shots must follow the available footage and spoken timing. The sequence above illustrates the reasoning, not a mandatory template.
